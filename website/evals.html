Training and evaluation

Training a two-level policy, then finding out which level failed

The policies we train have two levels. A high-level model (Molmo 2) looks at the camera and picks the next skill and a target point; a low-level model (ManiFlow) moves the arm. Both are trained only on ToyBlocks data. When an episode fails, we want to know which of the two was responsible, so the eval system lets you swap either one for an oracle.

Training

During data generation the planner reads every block's pose straight from the simulator. A trained policy doesn't get that. It has to work out where the blocks are from camera images, the same way it would on a real robot, so both models map raw camera images and the robot's joint state to actions.

We split the policy in two. Molmo 2, a vision-language model, is the high level: it chooses the skill to run and points at the block to run it on. ManiFlow is the low level and carries out that one instruction. ManiFlow is trained to pause once it has finished a skill and ask Molmo 2 for the next one.

Molmo2-4B-tb high level

  • Input: one 640×360 front-camera image and the goal as text.
  • Output: a short plan, then the next action and its target as a point in the image.
  • Actions: pick, place (on a block or on the table), flip (left, right, forward, backward), rotate (cw, ccw) and done.
  • Training: Molmo2-4B fine-tuned for 6,000 steps at batch size 256 on three-block stacking episodes. A quarter of each batch is general pointing data (PixMo-Points, PixMo-Count, CoSyn-Point) so the model keeps its general pointing ability. About 8 hours on 8 H100s.

Docs: Molmo 2 in evals

maniflow-tb low level

  • Input: front and wrist RGB at 224×224, a 9-D robot state, the action name, and a heatmap drawn around the high level's target point.
  • Output: chunks of 9-D absolute actions (7 joints, gripper, play mode). It predicts 50 steps and runs the first 25.
  • Model: flow matching with consistency training, a CLIP ViT-B/16 image encoder and a 12-layer DiT action head, 322.5M parameters.
  • Training: 200,000 steps on 8 GPUs, batch 128 per GPU, on three ToyBlocks-generated LeRobot datasets.

Prompt format

Molmo 2 gets the goal as a tower listed from top to bottom, followed by fixed instructions. It answers with the progress so far, the next step, and the action with its target point:

Promptstack A 4 B. plan. point to target. predict action.
Answernow: A not on 4, 4 on B. next: move A to 4. Do: block A <points coords="1 1 367 623">block A</points> pick
With historyprevious action: block A <points coords="1 1 367 623">block A</points> pick. stack A 4 B. plan. point to target. predict action.

The prompt can start with the previous step, written the same way as an answer. Point coordinates run from 0 to 1000 across the image width and height. Each target sits 5 px (in a 640×360 frame) below the block's center, on the face that points at the camera. ManiFlow then receives the action word ("pick") and a heatmap drawn around that point.

Pausing and play mode

ManiFlow doesn't just move the arm. It also learns to say "I'm done." ManiFlow's ninth action dimension is the play mode: 1 keeps going, 0 indicates to the model that the attempt is complete and the robot is retracting, and −1 says the skill is done. ManiFlow learns it from the training labels: when a pick, place, flip or rotate finishes the attempt and retract stages partway through a chunk, it emits a −1 and the rest of that chunk repeats the final pose and is marked as done. At eval time, 1 and 0 are fed back as part of the next state. A −1 ends the skill, and the arm holds where it stopped until the next query. The skill boundaries behind these labels come straight from data generation, where every frame is labelled with its sub-goal.

Molmo 2 can be queried on two schedules:

  • Synchronous (the default): Molmo 2 is queried at every action-chunk boundary, together with ManiFlow, so it can change its command in the middle of a skill.
  • Asynchronous: Molmo 2 is queried at the start of the episode and again only after ManiFlow reports −1. That needs far fewer high-level calls, and the command stays fixed for the whole skill. This mode needs a play-mode checkpoint.

Evaluation at Scale

Every eval episode starts from an episode in a dataset. An episode queue reads the robot pose, block poses and sizes, table height and goal from the recording, and the policy has to reach the goal from there. Since the scene comes straight from the dataset, a difference in success rate between two models reflects the models and not a difference in setup.

The queue hands episodes out to GPU-parallel environments, so the same command works on a single workstation GPU or across a multi-GPU machine.

TAMPER: training on your failures

TAMPER (TAMP Expert Relabeling) is a DAgger-style loop with a planning expert. An eval run finds the states where the policy goes wrong; the expert then plans new demonstrations starting from those states. The next round of training data then covers situations the policy actually ends up in, not only the ones data generation happens to sample.

  1. Evaluate with capture on. Turn on failure capture in an eval run. The final scene of every failed or timed-out episode is saved as a new environment package.
  2. Look at what was captured. A summary file lists each captured state with its episode, outcome and goal.
  3. Relabel. vecgen plans from the captured scene toward the recorded goal and executes it with the usual verified skills.
  4. Retrain and evaluate again. Annotate and merge the new episodes, add them to the training mix, and repeat.

In a small test on one RTX 5090, a random-action eval on a 16-episode stacking set captured 16 states, and vecgen recovered 12–13 of them in about 72 seconds.

Outputs

Each run scores every episode against its goal and writes per-step metrics and run summaries. Full rollouts, videos included, are recorded as a LeRobot dataset by default, in the same format as the training data. Any policy that can be served over a WebSocket can be evaluated by implementing a small policy-server interface.
