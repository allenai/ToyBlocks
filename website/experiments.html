VLA Spatial Generalization

Vision-language-action models (VLAs) use pretraining on large robot datasets. The purpose is that a VLA adapts to a new workspace with a small number of demonstrations. With ToyBlocks, we evaluate the spatial generalization of these action models in a simple setting. Each change in task space is meaningful and easy to reproduce. We change only the block positions or the stack height, and we keep all other conditions the same.

Setup

We use four tasks: Stack, Unstack, Flip and Rotate. Flip has four directions: forward, backward, left and right. Rotate has two directions: clockwise and counterclockwise. The instruction gives the direction.

We fine-tune three architectures: ManiFlow, π0.5 and MolmoAct 2. Each model gets 1,000 demonstrations for each task. The ToyBlocks TAMP planner makes these demonstrations at random positions in the training region. When the test region is the same as the training region, the mean success rate is 72% to 90%.

Then we move the test region. We also use three more splits. We train on the inner region and test on the outer region. We train on the outer region and test on the inner region. We train on stacks of 2 and 3 blocks and test on stacks of 4 and 5 blocks.

Surprisingly, these small changes cause large decreases in VLA performance. The models perform well in the training region. However, in the test region, their success rate is low, or they fail. This occurs although the test conditions are very similar to the training conditions. It also occurs although some of these models have pretraining on large datasets.

Five techniques

We tested five changes to the training. We added each change by itself.

  • Co-training. Half of each fine-tuning batch comes from background tasks. For each target task, the background tasks are the remaining three tasks. For example, for target task Stack (height 2 and 5), the background tasks are Rotate, Flip and Unstack (all heights). Background task demonstrations cover the full workspace, without table region or height limit.
  • Mid-training. Before fine-tuning, we train the model on a larger dataset. This dataset covers the full workspace, but it does not include the target task.
  • Delta actions. The model predicts each action chunk as changes from the current joint positions. It does not predict absolute joint targets.
  • World action model (WAM). Videos are available for the full workspace, but action labels are available only for the training region. For example, we give videos of the right half without actions. A video-prediction head (Fast-WAM style) trains together with the action expert. The rollout does not use this head.
  • LoRA. We freeze the model and train only low-rank adapter weights.

What matters

Keep some of the initial data. Co-training gives the largest single gain on the right half of the table (+45 points). Mid-training uses the same type of data only one time, before fine-tuning, and gives +14 points. Fine-tuning on one task also causes the model to forget the other tasks. Co-training with the data from these tasks prevents most of this loss. Thus, open robot data is important. A fine-tuned VLA gets its full generalization only when it continues to train on some of its initial data.

Predict delta actions. Delta actions give better results than absolute joint targets for π0.5 and for MolmoAct 2. On new stack heights, delta actions give +26 points. This gain does not come from a match with the pretraining action format. MolmoAct 2 used absolute joint actions in pretraining, and π0.5 used velocity actions.

Add video prediction when the scenes look new. By itself, WAM gives +3 points on the right half of the table and +23 points on new stack heights. WAM gives the best results together with co-training. We trained MolmoAct 2 on stacks of 2 and 3 blocks and tested it on stacks of 4 and 5 blocks. With WAM and co-training, its success rate increases from 29% to 80%. We think that the video head removes a visual limit, because the training images do not show stacks of 4 or 5 blocks.

Summary

Fine-tuned VLAs perform well in the training region, but small shifts in task space cause large decreases in success. We explored and evaluated many techniques in detail, and these techniques improve generalization in our simple settings. Nevertheless, VLA generalization is far from solved. ToyBlocks gives researchers a flexible and reproducible way to study new generalization techniques and more difficult settings.

Details. We use 1,000 training demonstrations for each task. Co-training and mid-training use 5,000 demonstrations for each background task, while keeping the 1,000 demonstrations for target task. We fine-tune for 30,000 steps. We evaluate each 10,000 steps and report the best checkpoint. Each run has 500 evaluation episodes. We normalize the joint actions to [−1, 1] with statistics from the full workspace, so that the robot can reach all regions.

For the setup of the evaluation episodes, refer to Evaluation at Scale.

On a real robot

We trained Molmo 2 and ManiFlow only on ToyBlocks data, with no real-world demonstrations, and ran them on a Franka FR3 with a Robotiq gripper. We tested two things: single skills with ManiFlow (pick, place, flip and rotate), and full three-letter spelling tasks with Molmo 2 + ManiFlow. Real-world runs used thirty scene configurations with 4–10 blocks that appear in the training data.

For both, the real-robot results are consistent with simulation. Both also need more trials before they can support any substantial claim. The spelling runs used the easiest setting, with every letter visible and upright.

How the transfer works: Sim-to-real Toolkit

Frontier-model agents

With the Agentic Framework, a frontier model (Claude Opus 5.5 in these runs) sees the camera images and calls ToyBlocks skills as MCP tools, pointing at the block it wants. It gets no success signal, so it has to decide for itself when the task is done, or when it can't be done.

30 / 30of the same tasks solved closed loop, choosing each action from camera images
63%of search-and-spell tasks solved, or correctly declared impossible
13%, 15%of search-and-spell tasks lost to look-alike letters, and to a letter stacked in the wrong orientation

Search and spell

The agent has to stack a three-letter word, finding the letters among up to eight blocks. In half of the scenes a letter is missing, and then the right answer is to say the task is impossible. On patterned blocks, each block carries one letter in several forms (upper and lower case, plus pictures that start with it), so the agent can work out what a hidden face must show. On unpatterned blocks the faces are unrelated. With patterns the agent finishes having turned over fewer faces, and on infeasible scenes it declares the task impossible much sooner.

Learning its own skill descriptions

When a skill behaves unexpectedly, the agent forms a hypothesis, runs a small test and revises its understanding, then writes its own descriptions of the skills. Those learned descriptions work better than the ones an expert wrote.

World model as a simulator

Action-conditioned world models predict how a scene changes in response to a robot's actions, and using them in place of model-based simulators is an active area of research. To replace these simulators, world models need accurate dynamics and spatial generalization. Because ToyBlocks knows the true state of every block, we can measure both.

Setup. We trained or fit three types of world models on four tasks (pick-and-place, flip, rotate, and a mixed task): video world models (Wan 2.2, Ctrl-World, WEAVER and IWS), an agentic model that created its own physics simulator (Astra), and latent world models that predict future latent states (LeWM and V-JEPA 2.1). Each task has 900 trajectories. Half succeed and half fail because the trajectory was changed slightly, so always predicting success is right only half the time. Each model gets an initial image and the actions. On full trajectories this is the first image of the episode; on short interaction segments (a single pick, place, flip, or rotate) it is the real image immediately before that interaction. To measure specific aspects of prediction accuracy, small probes trained on simulator labels read block pose, gripper pose and success from each prediction.

The best video world model has the best prediction accuracy. Wan 2.2 tracks the blocks to about a centimeter and predicts 99% of outcomes correctly. Ctrl-World and WEAVER are also accurate on short interaction segments (92% and 89%) but fall behind over full trajectories (69% and 52%).

The agentic approach tracks the robot well but struggles with contact dynamics. Astra follows the gripper closely (1.1 cm), but even with the true initial block poses its block error is about 6 cm and it predicts only 67% of outcomes.

Latent models capture short interactions but drift over long ones. LeWM predicts 65% of outcomes on short interaction segments, but over full trajectories the block error of both latent models grows to 24–25 cm.

World models help with extrapolation. Adding 100 Wan 2.2 rollouts from a part of the table with no real demonstrations raises a ManiFlow policy's success there from 19% to 78%.
